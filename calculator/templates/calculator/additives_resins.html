A) Polymer Processing Aid (PPA)
Function & Properties

Eliminates melt fracture (sharkskin) and reduces die build-up (DLBU), enabling:

  • Higher output rates
  • Lower extrusion temperatures/pressures
  • Improved film clarity and gloss
  • Longer run times between die cleaning
Mechanism & Substitutes

Conventional: Fluoropolymer-based PPA (Dynamar, Viton) PFAS

PFAS-free 1: Silicone / siloxane-based processing aids PFAS-free

PFAS-free 2: Polyethylene glycol (PEG) / polyester-based PPAs PFAS-free

PFAS-free 3: Boron nitride-based PPAs PFAS-free

Basic option: Metallic stearates (Zinc stearate) – weaker effect

Fluoropolymer migrates to the die surface, creating a low-energy lubricating layer that lowers shear stress. PFAS-free chemistries work by similar die-coating or slip-layer mechanisms but usually need longer conditioning and more optimisation.

Concentration Effects
Low Rate (500-800 ppm): Requires "conditioning" time but ultimately eliminates defects
High Rate (>1500 ppm): May increase film haze due to light scattering
Interaction: Silica/talc anti-block and acidic HALS can adsorb PPA and slow conditioning — add extra PPA or pre-condition the die.
Focus Polymers
LLDPE HDPE PP mLLDPE

Essential for high-molecular-weight resins with high shear sensitivity. mLLDPE and HDPE film grades gain the most.

Fluoropolymer PPAs are PFAS. See the PFAS section for why the move away is a necessity.
B) Slip Additive
Function & Properties

Reduces Coefficient of Friction (COF) to prevent:

  • Sticking to processing equipment
  • Blocking during converting
  • Difficulty opening bags
Mechanism & Substitutes

Primary: Erucamide (C22 unsaturated)

Substitute 1: Oleamide (C18 unsaturated)

Substitute 2: Stearamide (C18 saturated)

Fatty acid amides bloom to film surface over time, forming thin lubricating layer. Erucamide provides longer-lasting effect; oleamide acts faster but migrates more and is less heat-stable.

Concentration Effects
Low Rate (<800 ppm): High COF, severe blocking issues
High Rate (>2000 ppm): Too slippery, reduced heat seal strength, weak boundary layer, poor ink adhesion and lamination bond
Focus Polymers
LDPE LLDPE PP HDPE

Essential for packaging film handling and conversion processes. For printed or laminated film, keep slip low on the print/lamination side.

C) Anti-Block Additive
Function & Properties

Prevents blocking (film layers sticking together) and improves:

  • Film handling
  • Roll unwinding
  • Processing efficiency
Mechanism & Substitutes

Primary: Synthetic silica (diatomaceous earth)

Substitute 1: Natural talc (magnesium silicate)

Substitute 2: Calcium carbonate (precipitated)

Inorganic minerals create micro-roughness allowing air cushion between film layers. Synthetic silica offers best clarity.

Concentration Effects
Low Rate (<2000 ppm): Poor anti-blocking, film adhesion issues
High Rate (>10000 ppm): Drastic haze increase, reduced clarity, potential tear strength reduction
Focus Polymers
LDPE LLDPE HDPE PP

Critical for high-clarity and thin-gauge packaging applications. Slip + anti-block are normally used together: anti-block creates the spacing, slip lowers friction.

D) Anti-Fog Masterbatch
Function & Properties

Stops condensation from forming as tiny light-scattering droplets, keeping the film transparent:

  • Clear view of fresh produce, meat, and dairy
  • Less water droplet damage to packed product
  • Better light transmission in greenhouse/agricultural film
  • Reduced mould/bacterial growth from droplets falling on product
Mechanism & Substitutes

Primary: Glycerol esters (e.g. glycerol monooleate, GMO)

Substitute 1: Polyglycerol esters

Substitute 2: Sorbitan esters (Span) and ethoxylated sorbitan esters

Substitute 3: Ethoxylated amines/alcohols (long-term outdoor films)

These hydrophilic additives migrate to the surface and raise surface energy, so condensate spreads into a continuous transparent water film instead of beading. Delivered as a masterbatch for accurate, dust-free dosing.

Concentration Effects
Low Rate: Droplets reappear, short anti-fog lifetime
High Rate: Surface becomes greasy/wet, reduced heat-seal and print adhesion, additive wash-off, possible odour and migration concerns
Typical masterbatch dosing: about 1–3% (let-down) depending on masterbatch loading and application.
Focus Applications & Cautions
Fresh Produce Meat/Poultry Trays Dairy Lids Greenhouse Film
  • Place in the layer facing the humid side (usually inner/sealant layer in coex)
  • Competes with slip and antistat for surface — test combinations; amide slip may need to be reduced
  • Corona treatment and ink adhesion can drop — print the non-fog side
E) Base & Blend Resins: HDPE (HTA 108 / HD 6207FL), LLDPE and mLLDPE

These are not "additives" in the strict sense, but they are added to a film recipe in significant percentages to tune stiffness, toughness, sealing and barrier. Each one solves a different problem.

HDPE Film Grades – ExxonMobil HTA 108 / HD 6207FL

HTA 108: HDPE film grade designed to improve stiffness and barrier in coextrusion or in PE blends.

HD 6207FL: HDPE homopolymer film grade designed to improve stiffness, barrier properties and processability when coextruded or blended with LLDPE or mLLDPE.

Why they are added:

  • Stiffness (modulus): allows downgauging while keeping film rigidity for form-fill-seal and bag handling
  • Moisture barrier: crystalline HDPE has a lower water vapour transmission rate than LDPE/LLDPE — a key layer for dry foods and to shield EVOH/Nylon
  • Processability: high-density grades support strong bubble stability (high-stalk) and good output when blended with softer LLDPE/mLLDPE
  • Heat resistance: higher melting point improves sealing window and hot-fill resistance
Typical use: 10–30% in LLDPE/mLLDPE blends, or as a core/skin layer in coextrusion. Higher levels increase stiffness and barrier but reduce clarity, tear and dart impact, and make the film crinkly.
Substitutes & Related Options

Substitute 1: Other HDPE film grades (hexene/butene copolymer or homopolymer, different melt index)

Substitute 2: Medium-density PE (MDPE) – softer, better tear/clarity, less barrier

Substitute 3: PP layers (stiffness/heat resistance, but different sealing and recycling stream)

HDPE is not an oxygen barrier. It improves moisture barrier and stiffness only; use EVOH/Nylon for oxygen barrier.

Focus applications:

Bread/Bakery Bags Dry Food Liners Shopping Bags Heavy-Duty Sacks Coex Core Layers
LLDPE (Linear Low-Density PE)

Why it is added:

  • Toughness: high tensile, puncture and tear resistance vs LDPE, allowing downgauging
  • Impact strength: improves dart drop in HDPE and LDPE blends
  • Sealing: lower seal initiation temperature than HDPE, good seal strength
  • Cost-effective backbone: base of most stretch, shrink, liner and sack films

Substitutes: mLLDPE (higher performance), LDPE (easier processing, better clarity, lower strength), plastomers/POE.

Typical use: 20–80% in LDPE/LLDPE blends; up to 100% in many films. Higher melt strength sensitivity — blend 10–30% LDPE for bubble stability and/or use PPA.
mLLDPE (Metallocene LLDPE)

Why it is added:

  • Superior dart impact and puncture: narrow molecular weight distribution and uniform comonomer give best toughness at thin gauge
  • Lower seal initiation temp and strong hot tack: faster packaging line speeds, strong seals through contamination
  • Better optics: low haze and high gloss
  • Low extractables/odour: useful for food and medical film

Substitutes: Conventional Ziegler-Natta LLDPE (cheaper, lower toughness), plastomers (more seal performance, softer).

Typical use: 10–40% blended into LLDPE/LDPE, or 100% in sealant and high-performance layers. Harder to process (high pressure, lower melt strength) — blend 10–20% LDPE and use PPA (preferably PFAS-free).

F) Barrier Resins - EVOH & Nylon
EVOH (Ethylene Vinyl Alcohol)

Function: Superior oxygen barrier protection

  • Excellent gas barrier properties
  • High clarity and gloss
  • Oil and organic solvent resistance

EVOH Grades:

  • EVOH 32% - Standard barrier
  • EVOH 38% - High barrier
  • EVOH 44% - Ultra high barrier

Note: the % figure is ethylene content. Lower ethylene gives a stronger oxygen barrier but is stiffer, more moisture sensitive and harder to process.

Nylon (Polyamide) & Substitutes

Primary: Nylon 6 (PA6) - Good oxygen barrier, excellent toughness

Substitute 1: Nylon 66 (PA66) - Higher temperature resistance

Substitute 2: MXD6 Nylon - Enhanced barrier properties

Substitute 3: Amorphous Nylon - Better clarity

Nylon provides good oxygen barrier with excellent mechanical properties and puncture resistance.

Processing Considerations
EVOH: Highly hygroscopic - must be dried to <0.3% moisture; barrier drops sharply when wet, so protect with PE/HDPE layers
Nylon: Requires drying to <0.1% moisture to prevent degradation
Both: Sensitive to thermal degradation - control melt temperature
Applications
Food Packaging Medical Packaging Pharmaceutical Aroma-sensitive Products

Used in coextruded structures as core barrier layer

G) Tie Layers/Adhesives for Barrier Coextrusion
Function & Properties

Provides strong interlayer adhesion between incompatible polymers:

  • PE/PP to EVOH adhesion
  • PE/PP to Nylon adhesion
  • Critical for multilayer structural integrity
  • Enables complex barrier film structures
Primary Tie Layers & Substitutes

For EVOH:

  • Primary: PE-g-MA (Maleic Anhydride)
  • Substitute 1: EVA-g-MA
  • Substitute 2: Acid Copolymers

For Nylon (Bynel/Admer):

  • Primary: Bynel series (DuPont)
  • Substitute 1: Admer series (Mitsui)
  • Substitute 2: Orevac series (Arkema)
Mechanism & Concentration

Mechanism: Maleic anhydride groups react chemically with OH groups (EVOH) or amine groups (Nylon), forming covalent bonds during coextrusion.

Typical Use: 5-15% in tie layer, 3-8% in total structure
Focus Applications
EVOH Coextrusion Nylon Coextrusion Meat Packaging High-Barrier Films

Essential for multilayer barrier structures in food and medical packaging

Solvent systems shown are typical industry practice and illustrate why each resin family needs a different solvent balance. Always follow the ink supplier's TDS and run print/adhesion trials. NC = nitrocellulose, PU = polyurethane, PA = polyamide, CPP = chlorinated polypropylene, MPP = modified polypropylene.

Lesson learned: The NC-maleic variant of Topflex was discontinued because of bread bag adhesion failures. Surface-printed bread bags see handling, condensation and slip additive bloom — always validate ink adhesion with tape tests after film ageing.
Solvent Guide – Why Each Solvent Is Used
Ethyl Acetate

Role: Main active solvent

Strong solvency for NC, PU and vinyl; fast-medium evaporation; low odour compared to ketones. Works with alcohols as a diluent blend.

Ethanol / IPA

Role: Diluent / co-solvent

Fast-medium drying, cost-effective, dissolves PA. IPA is less aggressive; alcohols in laminating inks must stay low because of isocyanate reaction.

n-Propanol

Role: Retarder / PA solvent

Slower evaporation prevents cell drying and improves lay-down and gloss; key solvent for polyamide inks. Alcohol retention must still be controlled.

MEK (Ketones)

Role: Strong, fast solvent

Used for vinyl and some CPP systems on non-absorbent films. Strong odour and retention risks — limit in food packaging.

Glycol Ether Retarders

Role: Slow-evaporating retarder

Small amounts for long press runs, fine detail or heavy coverage to avoid dry-in. Overuse increases retained solvent and odour.

Retained Solvent Control

Why it matters

Residual solvent causes odour, taint, poor lamination bond and blocking. Match solvent blend to drying capacity, speed and film.

I) Overprint Varnish (OPV)
Function & Properties

A clear protective coating applied over the printed ink layer to:

  • Protect print from rub, scuff and scratch during converting and handling
  • Provide gloss, satin or matte finish
  • Control COF/slip on the printed face
  • Improve heat resistance so print does not stick to sealing jaws
  • Improve resistance to moisture, grease and chemicals
  • Help prevent blocking in the roll
Types & Substitutes

Primary: Solvent-based NC / NC-PU varnish – same solvent family as the ink (ethyl acetate, ethanol, IPA)

Substitute 1: Polyamide-based OPV (alcohol-based) – for PA ink systems

Substitute 2: Water-based acrylic OPV – low VOC, water with small alcohol fraction

Substitute 3: UV-curing OPV – very high gloss and resistance, needs UV curing equipment

Choose OPV chemistry to match the ink system (NC, PA, PU) so it adheres and does not lift or attack the print.

Common OPV Additives & Effects
  • Wax (PE, carnauba): rub resistance and slip. Too much hurts heat sealing and lamination
  • Silica matting agents: matte/satin finish (reduces gloss, can reduce clarity)
  • Defoamer/wetting agents: avoid pinholes and cratering
  • Crosslinker: chemical resistance for water-based acrylic grades
  • PTFE wax: avoid — fluoropolymer (PFAS)
Cautions
Over-slip OPV: bags slide off stacks, lose heat-seal strength and may cause label/adhesive failure.
Seal area: use heat-resistant OPV, or leave the seal area unvarnished, so jaws do not stick to the print.
Interaction: film slip additive blooming through thin ink/OPV can cause adhesion loss — test after ageing.

Conclusion: PFAS and Why the Shift to PFAS-Free Resins & Additives Is a Necessity
What are PFAS?

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a large family of fluorinated chemicals built on the extremely strong carbon–fluorine bond. That bond makes them heat- and chemical-resistant and highly slippery — and also makes them persistent ("forever chemicals"): they barely degrade in the environment, spread through water and soil and can accumulate in living organisms. Some PFAS are linked to adverse health effects, and several (PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS) are already restricted under the Stockholm Convention.

Where PFAS Appear in This Guide
  • Fluoropolymer PPAs (Dynamar, Viton type) in film extrusion
  • PTFE wax in inks and OPV
  • Fluorosurfactant wetting agents in inks/varnishes
  • Masterbatch carriers and recycled content that may carry fluorinated residues

Fluoropolymers are used at very low ppm, but they still count as PFAS in regulatory definitions and traces end up in the film, die deposits, scrap and wastewater.

Why the Shift Is a Necessity
  • Regulation: The EU is restricting PFAS — a broad restriction proposal is under evaluation, and the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation sets limits on PFAS in food-contact packaging. US states have also introduced PFAS bans in packaging.
  • Market access: Exporters and brand owners supplying EU/US markets increasingly require PFAS-free declarations and total-fluorine testing.
  • Supply security: Major fluoropolymer producers have announced exits from PFAS manufacturing, so supply and pricing of fluoropolymer PPAs are uncertain.
  • Brand and retailer policy: Many food and consumer brands have PFAS-free packaging commitments.
  • Health and environment: Persistence, bioaccumulation and contamination of water sources make precaution the responsible choice.
  • Recycling: PFAS in film complicates circular economy and recycled-content claims.
PFAS-Free Replacement Map
Application PFAS Product PFAS-Free Options Trade-offs

Ink / OPV wax PTFE wax PE wax, carnauba, paraffin, polypropylene wax Slightly lower rub/slip performance — tune dosage
Ink wetting agents Fluorosurfactants Acetylenic diol, silicone wetting agents May need more optimisation on low-energy films
Practical Transition Steps
  1. Audit every masterbatch, ink, varnish and additive for fluorinated components using supplier declarations and SDS.
  2. Request written PFAS-free statements from suppliers (including carriers and recycled content).
  3. Trial PFAS-free PPA; purge the die and screens of fluoropolymer residues beforehand.
  4. Re-optimise dosage, melt temperature, die gap and cooling to compensate for different performance.
  5. Test film: haze, gloss, seal strength, slip/COF and print adhesion after ageing.
  6. Verify with laboratory total-fluorine or PFAS analysis where required by customers.
  7. Document formulation changes and keep records for regulatory and customer audits.
Bottom Line
PFAS-free film production is now achievable with commercial alternatives, and the cost of waiting — lost market access, supply risk and regulatory exposure — is higher than the cost of re-qualifying a formulation.

Regulations and supplier positions are changing quickly. Confirm the current status of EU, US-state and customer requirements for your target markets before finalising formulations.

Key Takeaways & Selection Guidelines
Additive & Resin Selection Principles
  • HDPE (HTA 108 / HD 6207FL): add for stiffness, moisture barrier and bubble stability — typically 10–30%; accept some loss of clarity and tear
  • LLDPE / mLLDPE: add for toughness and sealing; mLLDPE gives best dart, hot tack and optics but needs LDPE and PPA help in processing
  • Anti-fog: put it in the humid-side layer, test with slip/antistat and print adhesion
  • EVOH: Choose ethylene content based on barrier requirements and flexibility needs
  • Tie Layers: Match chemistry to specific barrier resin (EVOH vs Nylon)
  • Inks: Match ink resin (NC, NC-PU, NC-PA, PA, vinyl, CPP/MPP) to substrate, print method (surface vs lamination) and end-use
  • OPV: Match varnish chemistry to ink and keep slip/wax in balance with sealing and lamination needs
  • Substitutes: Always test compatibility and performance before full implementation
Critical Processing Notes
  • EVOH/Nylon: MUST be properly dried to prevent degradation
  • Tie Layers: Require adequate layer distribution and contact time
  • Ink Additives: Balance components to avoid conflicts (wax vs adhesion)
  • Laminating inks: Keep residual alcohol and solvent low to protect isocyanate adhesive bonds
  • Barrier Films: Protect EVOH with hydrophobic layers from moisture
  • PFAS: Avoid fluoropolymer PPA, PTFE wax and fluorosurfactants; get written supplier declarations
Additive Interaction Checklist
  • Slip + anti-block: use together — spacing from anti-block, low friction from slip
  • Slip/antistat/anti-fog: all migrate to the surface and compete — over-dosing hurts seal strength, ink and lamination adhesion
  • PPA + silica/talc/HALS: may adsorb PPA — allow longer conditioning or higher PPA level
  • Slip bloom + ink: amides migrating through ink/OPV can cause adhesion failure (bread bag issue)
  • Recycled content: add antioxidant and check for contaminants including fluorinated residues
Quality Control Tests to Run
  • COF: static/kinetic after ageing (slip and anti-block)
  • Haze/gloss/clarity: after changing anti-block, PPA or HDPE level
  • Dart impact and tear: after HDPE/mLLDPE changes
  • Seal strength and hot tack: after slip, antifog, OPV or resin changes
  • Ink adhesion (tape test), rub test and retained solvent: fresh and aged samples
  • Moisture content: EVOH/Nylon before extrusion
  • Anti-fog test: cold-fog test at storage temperature
Professional Formulation Insight

Successful additive formulation requires understanding both chemical interactions and practical processing constraints. Always conduct trials to optimize additive levels for your specific equipment, materials, and product requirements. Maintain detailed records of formulations and their performance for future reference and troubleshooting. Design new formulations PFAS-free from the start rather than retrofitting later.
